01  The Week in Brief AI · Geopolitics · Tech · Funding

Five stories from the intersection of AI, geopolitics and the technology economy — curated and commented.

This week, the AI industry's structural tensions came into sharp relief: Apple sued OpenAI over alleged trade secret theft, signalling the collapse of one of tech's most consequential partnerships; companies across sectors quietly shifted to Chinese AI models to cut costs; and the SK Hynix Nasdaq debut — the largest foreign IPO in US history — confirmed that the memory chip market has become the fulcrum of the entire AI economy. The geopolitical and financial stakes of AI infrastructure have never been higher.

Apple Sues OpenAI Over Alleged Trade Secret Theft

— Financial Times

Apple has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI alleging the theft of top-secret information, with misconduct said to have been directed by senior OpenAI leadership including a longtime former Apple employee. The lawsuit marks the definitive breakdown of what was once a high-profile commercial partnership between two of Silicon Valley's dominant players.

Why it matters

A legal rupture between Apple and OpenAI reshapes the consumer AI landscape and raises serious questions about IP governance at the frontier of the industry.

The London lens

With Wayve, Synthesia, and ElevenLabs all building products that depend on partnerships with US AI labs, the Apple-OpenAI dispute is a reminder that IP and partnership terms between Big Tech and AI startups are increasingly fragile — and that UK companies relying on similar arrangements should be stress-testing their agreements now.

Companies Turn to Chinese AI Models to Cut Costs

— Financial Times

DoorDash, Siemens, and Airbnb are among the major corporations now deploying Chinese AI models in production to reduce ballooning inference costs and lower dependence on US providers. The shift is quiet but significant: enterprise adoption of Chinese models is accelerating precisely as US-China AI tensions dominate policy debate.

Why it matters

If Chinese models achieve cost and capability parity with US alternatives, the assumption that Western AI labs will dominate enterprise deployment dissolves — with major implications for the competitive moats of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google.

The London lens

UK companies operating under the National Cyber Security Centre's guidance on AI supply chain risk will need to square cost pressures against formal policy on using models from jurisdictions deemed high-risk — a tension that Brussels and Westminster have yet to resolve in clear regulatory language.

SK Hynix Raises $26.5 Billion in the Largest Foreign IPO in US History

— TechCrunch

South Korean memory chipmaker SK Hynix priced its Nasdaq IPO at $149, raising $26.5 billion and seeing shares jump 13% on debut — the single largest foreign listing in US market history. US officials are already pressing the company to commit to building domestic fabrication facilities.

Why it matters

The sheer scale of capital now flowing into AI memory infrastructure signals that HBM — the memory standard powering AI training and inference — has become as strategically contested as logic chips, and governments know it.

Tech context

HBM, or high-bandwidth memory, is the specialised chip stacked directly onto AI accelerators like Nvidia's H100 and B200. Without sufficient HBM supply, even the most advanced AI processors cannot run at full capacity — making SK Hynix and Samsung not peripheral suppliers but central bottlenecks in the entire AI compute chain.

OpenAI Launches GPT-5.6 Family as Leadership Vacuum Grows

— TechCrunch

OpenAI released GPT-5.6 and a new family of models this week, promising improvements in cybersecurity and confirming the family as the preferred engine for Microsoft Copilot 365. The release coincided with the departure of COO Fidji Simo, whose extended medical leave has left a significant leadership gap as OpenAI eyes an IPO and fights Anthropic for enterprise market share.

Why it matters

A model release combined with executive instability and an IPO on the horizon makes OpenAI's internal coherence — not just its technology — the key risk factor for partners and investors to watch.

Moneybox Picks London's PISCES Market for Employee Share Sale — Wayve Already Paved the Way

— Financial Times

Savings app Moneybox has chosen the UK's new PISCES private markets platform for a £45 million employee share sale, the second significant company to use the venue after autonomous vehicle startup Wayve became its first major participant. The move gives further validation to the UK government's attempt to create a credible pre-IPO liquidity mechanism for private company employees.

Why it matters

PISCES is the UK's most concrete attempt to retain high-growth tech companies before they list in New York — whether it achieves genuine scale will determine whether London can compete as a home for late-stage private capital.

The London lens

Wayve's early adoption of PISCES puts one of the UK's most prominent AI-autonomous vehicle companies at the centre of a policy experiment that matters for the entire London tech ecosystem — if PISCES attracts more names of Wayve's profile, it changes the calculus for founders considering early US listings.

Watch This

Meta's in-house AI chips enter production in September — if yields are acceptable, Meta's reduced dependence on Nvidia hardware will ripple through the GPU supply chain by Q1 2027 and compress Nvidia's pricing power with hyperscalers.

02  Minds Worth Following Essays · Long reads · Podcasts

The sharpest thing written, said or published this week — on AI, ideas, or anything worth thinking about.

MIT Technology Review

Anthropic Found a Hidden Space Where Claude Puzzles Over Concepts

Anthropic researchers built a tool called the Jacobian lens that allows them to observe what large language models are actually doing between receiving a prompt and producing a response — a layer of processing previously invisible to researchers. What they found is that models appear to work through concepts in a latent space before committing to output, a process that ranges from the mundane to the genuinely unsettling. The finding matters because it is the clearest empirical evidence yet that frontier models have internal reasoning structure that their creators did not explicitly design — which has direct implications for AI safety, alignment research, and how confidently anyone should trust model outputs in high-stakes settings.

Financial Times

Who Cleans Up After the Vibe-Coding Party?

The piece argues that the current enthusiasm for AI code-writing tools — tools that generate functional software quickly with minimal human oversight — is producing a vast and growing layer of poorly understood, inadequately documented code that someone will eventually have to maintain. The unsung human caretakers of the web's infrastructure, the people who patch, document, and sustain software over years, are being overwhelmed by the volume of AI-generated output flooding repositories and production systems. The central insight is a supply-side one: AI massively increases the rate at which code is created while doing nothing to increase the rate at which it is understood, and the gap between those two curves is where the next generation of technical debt is accumulating.

Financial Times

Quantum Is Exciting, But Watch Out for the Schrödinger's Cat Bounce

The piece is a sober corrective to the wave of quantum investment enthusiasm, arguing that fault-tolerant quantum computing — the version capable of solving problems that classical computers cannot — remains years away from commercial reality. The term 'fault tolerant' refers to a machine that is sufficiently error-free to produce reliable outputs, a threshold that current quantum hardware has not reached at useful scale. The author's concern is not that quantum is overhyped in the long run, but that the gap between today's noisy intermediate-scale devices and a genuinely fault-tolerant machine is being systematically underestimated by investors, creating a classic setup for a boom-bust correction.

03  Staying Human Body · Mind · The Analogue World

Your health, your attention, your life off the screen. Evidence-led and never preachy.

The Practice

The Case for Doing Less on Purpose: Structured Rest as a Cognitive Tool

The FT's reporting on AI adoption fatigue this week — employers pushing staff to use new tools, only to find productivity and morale declining — points to a well-evidenced problem: cognitive overload degrades the very executive function that complex work requires. Research in occupational psychology consistently shows that deliberate, scheduled periods of non-task-directed rest, sometimes called 'diffuse mode' time, are not idleness but active recovery for the prefrontal cortex. For professionals in senior roles whose work involves sustained judgment under uncertainty, the practical implication is concrete: blocking 20–30 minutes of unscheduled, screen-free time mid-afternoon is not a luxury but a maintenance requirement, comparable in effect to sleep's role in memory consolidation. The irony flagged by this week's reporting is that organisations mandating AI tool usage are simultaneously eliminating the cognitive slack that allows people to evaluate, question, and improve the output those tools produce.

The Edit
Pastry Diplomacy: How a Norwegian Chef Rewrote the Rules of Embassy Social Media

The piece traces how a single Norwegian chef's approach to posting food content from Washington DC's embassy circuit triggered a broader rethink of diplomatic social media strategy — the argument being that warmth, specificity, and genuine craft cut through institutional noise in ways that polished government communications never do. For anyone managing a personal or institutional brand in a sceptical environment, the lesson is the same: audiences respond to evidence of real skill and human detail, not to messaging.

The Real Mystery Behind Moana: After 1,700 Years, Why Did Polynesians Suddenly Sail East?

New climate data is being used to probe one of history's most striking navigational puzzles: why Polynesian voyagers, after a long period of geographic stillness, abruptly resumed eastward expansion across the Pacific. The piece is a reminder that the biggest human decisions — where to go, when to move — are rarely explained by a single cause, and that rereading old evidence through new data often produces more questions than answers.

AI Companies Are Throwing Museums a Lifeline. What Do They Want in Return?

Cash-strapped cultural institutions are accepting AI partnerships to fund visitor engagement tools, but the piece examines the uncomfortable trade embedded in that deal: AI companies gain access to curated, high-quality cultural data while museums risk compromising the trust and interpretive authority that are their core assets. For London's gallery and museum sector, which sits at the intersection of public funding cuts and AI industry interest, the terms of these arrangements will define what 'public cultural institution' means in a decade.
