01  The Week in Brief AI · Geopolitics · Tech · Funding

Five stories from the intersection of AI, geopolitics and the technology economy — curated and commented.

The AI industry is splitting along two fault lines simultaneously: a price war between OpenAI and Anthropic as Chinese models gain ground, and a governance crisis at OpenAI as Sam Altman pushes toward a blockbuster IPO. Underneath both stories runs a harder geopolitical question — whether open-source Chinese AI is the next vector of global influence, more durable than any tariff or trade restriction.

The next China shock will come from open-source AI

— Financial Times

Countries adopting Chinese open-source AI models will not just be importing technology — they will be absorbing Chinese standards, governance frameworks, and dependencies. The argument is that open-source distribution makes this harder to counter than closed proprietary exports, because there is no single chokepoint to block.

Why it matters

If Chinese AI models become the default infrastructure in the developing world, Beijing gains a form of soft-power leverage that no export control can easily reverse.

Tech context

Open-source AI models are released with publicly available weights — the numerical parameters that define how the model behaves — meaning any government or company can download, modify, and deploy them without ongoing permission from or payment to the original developer, making downstream control effectively impossible.

The London lens

The UK is actively courting Global South partnerships on AI governance and standards through bodies such as the AI Safety Institute. If Chinese open-source models pre-empt that agenda, the UK's ambition to shape international AI norms becomes significantly harder to realise.

OpenAI upheaval mounts as Sam Altman readies IPO push

— Financial Times

Executive exits and changes to OpenAI's safety team are unsettling staff as the company prepares for what is expected to be one of the largest technology listings in history. The internal turbulence arrives precisely as Anthropic investors are pricing a rival IPO at $2 trillion or more.

Why it matters

A destabilised safety function at the world's most watched AI company, timed to a high-stakes public listing, is the kind of governance risk that regulators in Brussels, Washington, and London are watching closely.

The London lens

The UK's AI Safety Institute has a formal information-sharing agreement with OpenAI. Any weakening of OpenAI's internal safety structures has direct implications for what the Institute can access and how credibly it can brief ministers.

OpenAI and Anthropic in price war as Chinese AI rivals gain ground

— Financial Times

OpenAI and Anthropic have released cheaper models in direct response to competitive pressure from Chinese AI companies including DeepSeek and Zhipu. The price war is compressing margins across the frontier AI market at exactly the moment both companies are preparing for public listings.

Why it matters

Sustained margin compression in frontier AI will force hard choices about where to cut costs, and safety and alignment research are historically the first casualties when commercial pressure intensifies.

Databricks raises $5B at a $190B valuation as investor demand dwarfed its ask

— TechCrunch

Databricks originally sought to raise $1 billion in its latest round; investors pushed $15 billion at the company, and it settled on $5 billion, valuing the data and AI infrastructure firm at $190 billion. CEO Ali Ghodsi framed the excess demand simply: AI is expensive, and the money is chasing the pipes, not the models.

Why it matters

A $190 billion valuation for an AI infrastructure company — larger than many sovereign wealth funds' total tech exposure — signals that capital markets have decisively concluded the 'enabler' layer of AI will capture more value than the model layer itself.

The London lens

Databricks has a significant London engineering presence and counts several FTSE 100 financial services firms among its European customers. The valuation re-prices the entire data infrastructure sector and will be felt in comparable UK companies seeking their next funding rounds.

Stripe set to acquire AI gateway startup OpenRouter for more than $7 billion

— TechCrunch

Stripe is reportedly acquiring OpenRouter, a startup that acts as a unified gateway allowing developers to route queries across multiple AI models from different providers, at a valuation above $7 billion. OpenRouter's CEO had described the company as 'Stripe for AI', and the acquisition would make Stripe the default financial and routing infrastructure layer for AI application developers.

Why it matters

If Stripe controls both AI payment flows and model-routing infrastructure, it becomes the invisible plumbing beneath a generation of AI products — a position of structural leverage that rivals AWS's early dominance in cloud.

Tech context

An AI gateway sits between a developer's application and multiple underlying models, handling authentication, cost optimisation, fallback routing, and billing in a single layer — removing the need to integrate separately with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and others.

Watch This

Natural gas prices in parts of the US could triple according to new forecasts — hyperscalers that locked in gas-powered data centre capacity to meet AI demand may find their energy cost assumptions badly wrong within two to three quarters.

02  Minds Worth Following Essays · Long reads · Podcasts

The sharpest thing written, said or published this week — on AI, ideas, or anything worth thinking about.

Financial Times

AI vs the people

Historian Jill Lepore examines the growing grassroots opposition to AI data centres — noise, water consumption, electricity demand, land use — and argues that this resistance is not simply NIMBYism but a coherent political phenomenon with historical precedent. Drawing on earlier battles over railways, oil infrastructure, and nuclear power, she argues that how societies regulate transformative technologies is shaped less by technocrats than by ordinary people who bear the costs first. The central insight is that the current AI buildout is creating the same class of aggrieved local constituencies that historically forced the regulatory frameworks that constrained earlier industrial revolutions.

MIT Technology Review

What happens when a kid's robot best friend dies?

This piece follows children who formed genuine emotional attachments to Moxie, a therapeutic robot companion designed to help kids with anxiety and social development, and explores what happens to those children when the product is discontinued and the robot effectively 'dies'. The central argument is that as AI companions become sophisticated enough to be genuinely useful to vulnerable people, the commercial and ethical obligations of their makers need to catch up — a product shutdown is not a neutral business decision when a child has spent years building a relationship with the product. It raises a question that the AI industry has not seriously grappled with: what duty of care do companies owe when their technology becomes emotionally load-bearing?

TechCrunch

Anthropic CEO says AI backlash is 'fundamentally a crisis of trust'

Dario Amodei pushes back against the criticism that he has been painting an unnecessarily dark picture of AI's risks, arguing that the public resistance to AI is not irrational fear but a rational response to an industry that has repeatedly overpromised and under-explained. His core claim is that the problem is not the message — it is that the institutions delivering it have not yet earned the credibility to be believed. For senior professionals navigating AI adoption inside organisations, this reframe matters: resistance from colleagues and boards is more often a trust deficit than a knowledge deficit.

Quote of the week

“The AI backlash is fundamentally a crisis of trust. People aren't opposed to the technology — they're opposed to being asked to trust institutions that haven't demonstrated they deserve it.”

— Dario Amodei, CEO, Anthropic
03  Staying Human Body · Mind · The Analogue World

Your health, your attention, your life off the screen. Evidence-led and never preachy.

The Practice

The case for a hard morning boundary on news and notifications

A growing body of research on cortisol and cognitive load suggests that checking news, email, or social media within the first hour of waking materially increases baseline anxiety levels for the rest of the day — not because the content is necessarily stressful, but because the brain is primed for threat-detection in the early morning and encodes incoming information with greater emotional weight during that window. Neuroscientist Andrew Huberman has repeatedly cited this in the context of the morning cortisol spike, which peaks naturally within 30–60 minutes of waking and is best used for alert, focused work rather than reactive information processing. For professionals whose working days are largely reactive — responding to what the world throws at them — the morning hour is the single window where they hold genuine cognitive sovereignty. Protecting it is not a wellness luxury; it is a performance decision.

Via Huberman Lab  ·  Andrew Huberman, Stanford University
The Edit
The art of the goodbye message

This FT essay uses retirement announcements as a lens on a much harder question: why successful people find it almost impossible simply to stop. The insight is that the farewell message is rarely about the audience — it is a last act of identity management by people who have spent decades defining themselves entirely through professional achievement.

Building a practical path to post-quantum cryptography

MIT Technology Review cuts through the noise on quantum computing's threat to encryption, arguing that post-quantum cryptography is a manageable business evolution rather than an existential crisis — and that the organisations most at risk are those treating it as a distant problem rather than a current migration project.

Smoke, social media and the shame of the perfect holiday shot

Using wildfire smoke as a backdrop, this FT piece examines how environmental disaster has started to crack the grammar of the curated holiday image — and what it reveals about the gap between the experiences people actually have and the ones they feel compelled to perform online.
